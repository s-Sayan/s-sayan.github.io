Focal length and field of view

How the distance between the optics and a fixed-size detector sets both how much sky you see and how large it looks, first for a single lens and then for a two-mirror telescope.

A single lens

The sensor has a fixed size. Changing the focal length moves it closer to or farther from the lens. Trace a ray from each edge of the sensor back through the center of the lens: the angle between those rays is the field of view.

FOV = 2 · arctan(d / 2f)

A short focal length puts the sensor close, so it accepts a wide cone and each object covers few pixels. A long focal length puts it far back, so the same pixels share a narrow patch of sky and each object covers more of them. Field of view and magnification are the same geometry seen from two sides.

A two-mirror telescope

The concave primary alone would focus light at its focal length f₁, the prime focus. The convex secondary intercepts the converging beam before it gets there and slows its convergence, so the beam comes to focus much farther away, behind a hole in the primary. Treating the prime focus as a virtual object for the secondary:

p = f₁ − d,   s = d + b,   m = s / p,   F = m · f₁

Here d is the mirror separation, b the distance of the final focus behind the primary, m the secondary magnification, and F the effective focal length. Extend the final converging ray backward until it meets the incoming ray at its original height: the crossing point lies a distance F in front of the focus. A single lens placed there would give the same cone and the same image scale, so to the detector the folded telescope is indistinguishable from a long refractor.

Everything from the single lens carries over with f replaced by F: plate scale is 206265 / F and FOV ≈ detector size × plate scale. The secondary multiplies the focal length by m, so each side of the field shrinks by 1/m and the sky area by 1/m², while objects grow by m on the detector. The detector size sets how much focal plane you collect; the optics set how many arcseconds land on each millimetre.

  • Secondary size. A larger m, with the secondary closer to the prime focus, needs a smaller secondary because the beam is narrower there.
  • Focus sensitivity. The final focus moves by about m² + 1 times any motion of the secondary. At m ≈ 4, a 10 µm despace moves focus by about 170 µm, which is why thermal changes in separation matter and why focus is usually adjusted at the secondary.
  • What this leaves out. These are paraxial numbers. Off-axis aberrations such as coma and field curvature limit the usable field of a two-mirror design; Ritchey–Chrétien hyperboloids or a corrector recover image quality across the field without changing F or the plate scale.
